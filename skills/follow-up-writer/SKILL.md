# Follow-Up Writer

Turn messy notes into the email the other person actually wants: proof you listened, the thing you promised, and one easy next step. Most follow-ups fail by being about the sender ("great to meet you, here's what we do") instead of about the conversation.

## Inputs

Anything: bullet notes, a voice-memo transcript, a business card + "we talked about X", or just the user describing the conversation. If the notes don't say what (if anything) was promised or what the desired next step is, ask those two questions — they're the spine of the email. Everything else can be worked around.

## Method

1. **Extract from the notes:** who they are, the one topic they cared most about, anything the user promised (an intro, a link, an answer, a quote), and the natural next step.
2. **Lead with the conversation, not the greeting.** The first line should reference the specific thing discussed — never "It was great meeting you at [event]!" as a standalone opener; if that phrase appears, it earns its place by being attached to the specific moment ("Great talking ice-dams at the ServiceWorld booth —").
3. **Deliver the promise** in the email itself if possible (the link, the answer, the intro cc'd), not "I'll send that over soon."
4. **One next step, stated as the ask.** Not three options. Make it small and answerable from a phone: a yes/no, a 15-minute slot, a "want the longer version?"

## Output format

```
SUBJECT: [specific to the conversation, under 7 words, no "Following up"]

[Body — under 120 words, 2-3 short paragraphs, no bullet points unless
delivering a promised list. Sign-off matches the relationship's formality.]

— note: [anything the user must fill in or verify before sending, if any]
```

Offer one alternate subject line. If the notes support it, offer a shorter "phone-typed" variant (under 50 words) — conference follow-ups sent same-day from a phone often land better than polished ones sent Thursday.

## Quality bar

- The recipient could not receive this exact email from anyone else the user met that day.
- No "just checking in", "touching base", "circling back", or "hope you're well."
- Nothing invented: if the notes don't say they agreed to a call, the email can't claim they did.

## Example

Notes: *"met dana hvac ops person, hates her dispatch software, promised to send the article about the truck thing, she said email her"*

```
SUBJECT: The dispatch-vs-trucks article

Dana — here's the piece I mentioned on replacing a truck with better
dispatching: [link]. The section on driver pushback is the part that made
me think of what you said about your ops team.

If it lands, I'd happily show you how we handle the same problem — 15
minutes, no slides. Worth a look?

[name]

— note: paste the real article link before sending.
```
